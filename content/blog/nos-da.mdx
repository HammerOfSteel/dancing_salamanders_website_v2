Welsh has a goodnight that is softer than the English version. *Nos da*. Good night. It rolls off without much ceremony, the kind of phrase you say a hundred times without thinking about it, the thing that ends days the way a period ends a sentence, functional and unremarkable.

And then one day it means everything. And you cannot say exactly when that happened.

## The Ordinary Architecture of Days

What I have learned, in the years since, is that the moments we most need to have paid attention to are almost never the moments we recognise as important at the time. The important moments announce themselves. Birthdays. Firsts and lasts. The things that happen with witnesses and cameras and the sense that this is a thing to be marked.

The other moments don't announce anything. They arrive in the ordinary run of an evening. Bedtime. The lamp on the table. The particular way a small person looks when they have been fighting sleep for forty minutes and have finally, reluctantly, given in. The weight of them when they stop holding themselves up.

You say *nos da*. They say it back, or they don't, because they are already most of the way gone. You leave the room. The door is open a crack. You go back to whatever the evening was before bedtime.

That's all it is. That is the whole of it.

## Thresholds

Welsh folklore has always taken seriously the idea of the threshold. The doorway between inside and outside, between the day and the night, between this world and whatever else there is. The threshold is not a neutral space. It is a place of significance. You say certain things at thresholds and you do not say other things. The crossing matters.

*Nos da* is a threshold word. A threshold moment. The small ceremony that marks the boundary between wakefulness and sleep, between the child present and the child elsewhere, in whatever country sleep is.

I did not think about this at the time. You don't. You say *nos da*, *daffodil*, and you mean it the way you mean all the ordinary things, which is completely and without awareness. The meaning is bone-deep but it isn't conscious. You are not, in the moment, noting it down.

## What You Cannot Prepare For

There is no way to be prepared for the last ordinary moment, because its nature is to be ordinary. It comes and goes like all the others. It is only later, from a particular distance, that you look back and find the seam in the ordinary run of days and see that there, there was the last one.

I have thought about this and I don't think I would want to know, if I could. I don't think I would want the last *nos da* to have been a thing I was watching myself say, a thing I was performing rather than living. The gift of ordinary moments is exactly that they are not performances. They are just the days, the real days, the texture of an actual life.

The grief of them comes later. But so does this: the understanding that the texture was real. That it happened. That for all the evenings it was said without ceremony, it was also felt, it was also true, it was also the whole of love in three syllables.

## Still

I say *nos da* still. Not to no one. To the room, sometimes. To the dark.

This is not mysticism. I am not claiming anything about where words go when we say them, or whether they reach anyone. I say it because it is mine, because it belongs to a real thing that happened, because the small threshold ritual of it is one of the ways I carry what I carry.

Welsh has a concept of *cofio*, to remember, but also to call to mind, to bring back. The calling is an action. *Cofio* is something you do, not just something that happens to you.

*Nos da* is *cofio*. It is me calling to mind, on the near side of the threshold, the person I said it to.

It is enough. It has to be enough, and it is.
