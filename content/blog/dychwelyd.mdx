The Welsh word *dychwelyd* means to return. To come back. But Welsh has a way of loading its verbs with more than English usually carries, and *dychwelyd* is not simply the reverse of leaving. It is its own action. It asks its own things of you.

I drove up the valley on a grey Tuesday in October. Not a dramatic day. No particular ceremony to it. The M4 to Cardiff, then north through Caerphilly, then the narrowing of the road as the valley closes in on either side and the hills rise up and the whole landscape changes register — from the flat coastal plain to something older and more vertical, the kind of terrain that was made by glaciers and then softened over centuries by rain and vegetation and the stubborn persistence of people building terraced houses on slopes that would have discouraged sensible planning.

The Ebbw was still there. Of course it was. Rivers don't wait.

## What You Expect

Before you return to a place you have been away from for a long time, you form an image of what you will find. The image is assembled from memory and from the knowledge that things change, and the result is a kind of negotiated expectation — somewhere between the place you left and the place you know it must have become.

What you are not prepared for is the specific quality of being wrong in both directions at once.

Some things are unchanged in a way that is almost unsettling. The same pub on the corner. The same hill. The same particular angle of the light at half four in the afternoon in autumn, the sun already low over the ridge, the valley already beginning its slow slide into evening. That light is not a general thing. It belongs to this specific valley, this specific latitude, this specific relationship between the topography and the time of year. Nothing I had seen in the intervening years had that exact quality. And there it was again.

And other things are gone, or changed past recognition, or simply absent in the way that makes you realise you had been counting on them without knowing it.

## The Difference

The other thing I had not prepared for was myself.

When I left, I was one kind of person. A person with a particular weight of things behind them and a particular shape of loss that had not yet had years to settle into something that could be carried. I had been away long enough that the person who left and the person who came back share a name and a body and most of a history, but they are not the same.

This is not a complaint. It is just a fact that returning makes visible in a way that ordinary continuous living doesn't. When you stay in a place, the changes in you and the changes in the place happen at the same pace, and neither seems particularly dramatic. When you leave and come back, you see both at once, and the gap is visible.

There is a Welsh concept of *hiraeth* that most people outside Wales know as a kind of homesickness. But there is another word, *atgof*, which is simply memory, the thing you carry. Not the longing for it but the carrying of it. *Dychwelyd* is what happens when the *atgof* meets the actual place again, and the two have to negotiate.

## What the Valley Holds

I was not expecting it to be peaceful.

I had associated this valley, for years, with loss. Not with blame, not with bitterness, but the loss was here, was woven into the geography of it. The streets where she walked. The hills above Cwmcarn where we walked, her hand in mine, her commentary on everything, her certainty about which mushrooms were fairy houses and which were simply mushrooms.

What I found instead was that the valley holds the loss the way land holds water — not by containing it but by giving it somewhere to go. The grief doesn't disappear here. It doesn't need to. It has room.

There is something about being in the landscape that held someone that is different from carrying them in a landscape that never knew them. The hills above Crosskeys knew her. The Ebbw knew her. The particular slope of the road down into the town from the east, the way the valley opens out and you can see all the way down to the flat of the Usk basin on a clear day — these knew her, and in some way that I can't quite rationalise but have stopped trying to argue myself out of, they still do.

*Dychwelyd*. Coming back.

It is not a completion. It is not a healing. It is the start of learning to be in the place where everything happened, and finding that the place will have you, and that this is enough to be going on with.
