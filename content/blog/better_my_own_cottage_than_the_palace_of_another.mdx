The Welsh word *cartref* means home, but it carries more than the English does. *Cart* is a base, a foundation. *Tref* is a town, a settlement, a place where people have chosen to be. Together they suggest a home that is also a position, a place where you have located yourself in the world.

A terraced house in Crosskeys is a specific kind of *cartref*. Two up, two down, a back garden that goes further than you expect, the hill rising steeply behind it. The walls are solid. The ceilings are low. You feel the house around you in the way you feel a coat, something that fits your particular shape and no one else's.

## What Small Rooms Do

I have lived in a lot of rooms in the years since. Tokyo. Seoul. Kyoto. Stockholm. Each city had its own quality of room, its own scale, its own relationship between the inside and the outside. Some of them were beautiful. Some of them were comfortable. None of them were a *cartref*.

There is something a small room does that a large room doesn't. A small room is an argument for presence. It says: you are here, specifically, in this space, and the space knows you are here. A large room is more indifferent. A large room doesn't particularly require your presence to complete it.

The terraced house in Crosskeys is small in the right way. The kitchen requires you to be in it, not merely adjacent to it. The living room has room for you and for the things that belong to you and not much else. This is not a limitation. This is the house making a claim: be here. Actually be here, not somewhere nearby.

## Coming Back to It Alone

The thing nobody tells you about returning to a place that held more people than you now are is that the house doesn't scale down. It stays the same size. You don't take up more of it just because you need to.

I have spent some time finding what that actually means in practice. It means the second cup stays in the cupboard. It means the sound of the house at night is different from the sound of a house that is full of breath and sleep and the particular acoustic of other people. It means learning, again, the specific geography of solitude, which is different from loneliness, though it can feel like it in the first weeks.

Solitude is a condition. Loneliness is a response to it. The difference is in whether you resist the aloneness or sit with it long enough to find out what it is.

The house has been patient with me on this. I have not always been ready to sit with it. The house waited.

## What It Holds

A house that has been loved becomes a kind of archive.

Not in a sentimental way, not the way people mean when they say a place has good energy or holds the past. I mean something more plain: the decisions made in a house leave traces. Where things are placed, what is kept and what is let go, the particular arrangement of a room over years. It is all choices, and the choices accumulate.

The Crosskeys house holds choices from before I left and choices from after I came back, and the overlap between them is interesting to live inside. Some things are exactly where they were. Some things are gone, replaced, or rearranged to fit who I am now rather than who I was then. The two versions of the house coexist. They don't contradict each other.

## Enough

*Cartref*. The base in the town. The position in the world.

This is what I have come back to: a position. Not a resolved life, not a fixed point from which nothing will shift again, but a specific place in the world where the walls know me and the hill is familiar and the particular quality of the light in the mornings when the cloud sits in the valley and turns everything soft and grey and entirely itself is available to me every day if I remember to look.

It is enough to be here. More than enough. After years of rooms that didn't know me, it is, in ways I am still learning to articulate, a great deal.

The second cup stays in the cupboard. I drink my one cup at the kitchen table and look at the hill.

I am here. The house knows it.
