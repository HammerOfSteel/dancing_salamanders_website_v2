Perhaps you're reading this in the middle of the night. Perhaps it has been a few days, perhaps a few weeks, since everything changed — and you're sitting with your phone trying to understand what is happening inside you. You recognise yourself less with each passing day. You're not crying at all, or you never stop. You're angry, hollow, ice-cold, falling apart — sometimes all of it within a single hour. And somewhere the question creeps in: is this how you're supposed to feel? Is something wrong with me?

We want to say this before you read another word: no. Nothing is wrong with you. You are in grief. And grief after someone who took their own life has many faces — more than most people imagine before they find themselves standing in the middle of it.

Those of us writing this have ourselves lost someone to suicide. We recognise much of what's coming now. Not because your grief is exactly like ours — it isn't, grief is as personal as love — but because so many of us have been through similar storms and survived them. Here are some of the reactions we often carry, so you can recognise yourself and breathe a little.

## The Shock and the Unreality

In the beginning, the body often can't take in what has happened. That's actually a kind of grace. Many describe the first days and weeks as walking behind glass — you hear what people say, you answer, you might even organise practical things, but everything feels unreal. As though it will soon turn out to be a mistake.

You might catch yourself waiting for the person to come home, call, open the door. You might reach for your phone to tell them something — and remember in the same moment. Those small collisions with reality hurt every time, and they can go on for a long time.

The feeling of unreality isn't denial in any bad sense. It's the psyche letting in the truth at the pace it can manage. You don't need to force the realisation faster than it comes.

## The Anger — Even at the Person Who Died

Anger is one of grief's most misunderstood sides. You can be angry at the healthcare system, at yourself, at people who say the wrong things, at the whole unjust world. And you can be angry at the person who died. How could you leave me? Why didn't you ask for help? Why weren't we enough?

Many feel deep shame about that anger. You think you're not allowed to be angry at someone who suffered so much, someone who is dead and can't defend themselves. But listen: anger and love don't cancel each other out. To be furious at someone and miss them so much it aches — you can do both at once. Anger is often love with nowhere to go. It's okay. It doesn't mean you loved less.

## The Guilt and All the What-Ifs

Almost all bereaved carry guilt. The thoughts grind: what if I'd called that evening. What if I'd seen the signs. What if I'd said something different. The mind plays back the same scenes over and over, looking for the point where everything could have gone another way.

We want to say this clearly, even if it takes time to really believe it: suicide never has a single simple cause, and the responsibility wasn't yours. You're a person who loved another person. You couldn't read minds, and love unfortunately can't always reach through the darkness of illness. The guilt is real — but a feeling is not a verdict. We write more about this in a separate piece, because it's too heavy and too important to dismiss in one sentence.

## The Relief Nobody Talks About

This may be the hardest thing to acknowledge, so we say it carefully: sometimes there is also relief. If your person suffered for a long time, if you lived with mental illness for years — with constant worry, with calls in the night and a dread that never quite let go — then in the middle of grief there can be a small, quiet feeling that the endless waiting is over.

And then comes the shame of having felt it.

We want to say: relief doesn't make you cold or loveless. It means you carried something extraordinarily heavy for a long time. You can grieve deeply and feel relief at the same time. Both are allowed.

## When the Body Remembers

Grief doesn't live only in thoughts. It settles in the body. Very many of us notice that sleep stops working — you can't fall asleep, or you wake at three in the morning and are met by everything at once. Memory becomes like a sieve; you forget words, lose the thread, walk into rooms and don't know why. You're exhausted in a way no amount of sleep seems to fix.

There can be pain in the chest, the stomach, the shoulders. Appetite disappears or goes into overdrive. The immune system falters. All of this is the body's way of carrying something immense. It doesn't mean you're going mad — it means you're a person who has been deeply wounded.

Grief is not a sign that you're broken. It's the proof that you loved.

## Waves, Not Stages

You may have heard about the "stages of grief" — that you move from denial to anger to acceptance in a tidy order. For most of us, that's not at all how it is. Grief comes in waves. One moment you can feel almost whole, laugh at a memory, believe the worst is over — and the next day something as small as a smell or a song in a shop brings you to the ground.

That doesn't mean you're "going backwards" or doing it wrong. Waves are part of it. Over time the gaps between them often grow longer, and you learn to recognise them before they wash over you. But it's not a tidy staircase upward. It's an ocean. And you slowly learn to swim in it.

## You're Not Strange — You're Grieving

If you take only one sentence from this piece, let it be this: everything you feel right now, however contradictory and frightening, fits within what it is to grieve someone lost to suicide. Shock, anger, guilt, relief, emptiness, the strange sensation of laughing and then breaking — these are not signs that you're falling apart. These are grief's many faces.

And you don't have to face them alone.
