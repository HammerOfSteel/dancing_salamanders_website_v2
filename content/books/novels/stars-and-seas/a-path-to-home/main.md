# Part One: The Cliffs

The bed was too big for a man and a girl, but they filled it anyway.

Tima had colonised the centre — the way she always did, the way small children claim territory without negotiation, their bodies spreading outward from wherever they land until the available surface is theirs and the adults are pressed to the edges like footnotes. She lay diagonal across the duvet, one leg thrown out at an angle that defied the structural limitations of a two-and-a-half-year-old's hip joint, her dark curls fanned across the pillow in a pattern that looked, if you squinted, like a weather system seen from above. Hoppy was face-down beside her. He'd had a long day.

Papa lay on his side at the edge, watching her.

She was not asleep. She was in that state — the state that only toddlers truly master — where the body has committed to being horizontal but the mind has not yet received the memo. She was talking. Not to Papa, not to Hoppy, not to anyone in particular. She was talking to the ceiling, or to the room, or to whatever audience exists in the imagination of a child who has been told it is bedtime and has decided that bedtime is a suggestion rather than a fact.

"Hoppy doesn't want sleep," she announced, pressing the hippo's face into the pillow and then lifting it again. "He wants a story."

"Hoppy's had three stories."

"He wants four."

"Nobody needs four stories."

"Hoppy does. He's special."

Papa smiled. She'd picked that up from Eira — the special defence, the trump card that four-year-olds deploy with the certainty of a barrister citing precedent. What Eira said, Tima learned. What Tima learned, Tima used. The transmission was flawless and constant, a small-scale cultural exchange between two girls who shared no blood but shared everything else.

"Hoppy can have his fourth story tomorrow," Papa said.

Tima considered this. She rolled onto her side, facing him, and brought Hoppy up between them so that the hippo's one remaining eye — loose, purple, threatening to detach at any moment — was looking directly into Papa's face.

"Hoppy says okay."

"That's very generous of Hoppy."

She smiled — that smile, the one that started at the corners and moved inward, the wrong direction, as if the smile was arriving from somewhere else and her face was simply the place it landed. Storm blue eyes. Dark lashes. The face that contained, somehow, both of them — her mother's bones and her father's attention, the geometry of one and the stillness of the other.

She climbed onto him. Not gracefully — toddlers do not climb gracefully, they climb the way they do everything, with the full commitment of their weight and the absolute faith that the surface beneath them will hold. She clambered across his chest, one knee in his ribs, one hand grabbing his ear for balance, and then she was there — lying on top of him, her cheek against his sternum, her body small and warm and heavy in the way that only sleeping-adjacent children are heavy, as if gravity increases by a factor of three once the eyes start closing.

"Night night, Tad," she murmured.

"Nos da, daffodil."

Her breathing changed. That's how you knew with Tima — not the eyes, which could fool you, staying open long after the rest of her had surrendered, but the breathing. It slowed. It deepened. It found that particular rhythm — the in-and-out of a child who has finally, reluctantly, agreed to leave the day behind.

Papa held her. His hand on her back, feeling the small ribs expand and contract, the machinery of her working its quiet, reliable cycle. The room was dim. The curtain was drawn but the summer light leaked through — August in South Wales, the light that lingered past nine, that clung to the edges of the day like a guest who doesn't want to leave.

He was tired. The day had been long in the way that days with a two-and-a-half-year-old are long — not with events but with attention, with the sustained, high-resolution focus that parenting a small child requires, the constant scan, the perpetual readiness, the body tuned to the frequency of another person's needs as if his nervous system had been recalibrated at her birth and now operated on her signal rather than his own.

He closed his eyes. Tima's weight on his chest. The warmth of her. The sound of her breathing and, behind it, the sound of the valley — the distant murmur of the Ebbw somewhere below, the birds settling, the particular hush that Crosskeys made at dusk when the houses tucked themselves into the hillside and the trees on the ridge stood black against the pale sky.

He fell asleep before she did.



The dream was old.

It was not the kind of dream that arrives as narrative — no beginning, no middle, no sequence of events that the sleeping mind assembles into story. It was the other kind. The kind that arrives as sensation. As a photograph held underwater, its edges dissolving but its centre still sharp, still insisting on itself.

Wind. That was the first thing. Wind on his face — not cold, not warm, but present, physical, the wind as a hand pressing gently against his skin. And then the smell: salt. Not the English salt, not the Bristol Channel brine that arrived in Crosskeys on certain days when the weather backed up the estuary and pushed the sea-smell inland through the valley. Different salt. Cleaner. Older. The salt of the Baltic, of a sea that was barely a sea at all, more a lake with ambitions, shallow and cold and patient.

Cliffs. Not the Cornish kind — not the dramatic, crumbling, surf-hammered cliffsides that Peter talked about, the cliffs that fell away in sheets and sent houses tumbling into the Atlantic. Not the Tenby kind either — the painted-postcard cliffs with their candy-coloured houses perched above the harbour. These were Swedish cliffs. Gotland cliffs. Limestone. The raukar — the sea stacks that stood in the water like ancient sentinels, their shapes carved by millennia of tide and patience into forms that looked human if you caught them right, that looked like gods or trolls or the petrified remains of people who had stood at the water's edge too long and been claimed by it.

He was being held.

That was the centre of the dream, the part that the water could not dissolve. Arms. Large hands. The sensation of being lifted, of the ground leaving his feet and the sky coming closer and the world rearranging itself around the fact of being carried. He was small. Impossibly small. One year old, maybe less — the age before memory is supposed to begin, the age that the brain records anyway and stores somewhere beneath the architecture of conscious recall, in the basement, in the foundations, in the place where the body keeps the things it knew before it knew how to know.

His father's face.

This was the part that always arrived last, the part that the dream held back the way a storyteller holds back the final line — not for drama but for weight, for the particular gravity that accrues to things that are rare. His father's face, above him, looking down. The eyes — what colour were they? The dream would not say. The dream offered the shape of the face, the fact of it, the presence above him that was warmth and safety and the particular quality of being known by someone whose blood you share, but it withheld the details. The details were the price of being one year old when the knowing happened. You get the feeling. You lose the resolution.

They were on Gotland. He knew this the way you know things in dreams — not because anyone said it, not because there was a sign or a map, but because the dream told his body and his body believed it. Gotland. The island in the Baltic. Fårö, perhaps, or somewhere on the western coast where the raukar stood in their patient rows and the wind came in off the water and the light had that particular Scandinavian quality — flat, endless, the light of a latitude that holds the sun at arm's length even in summer.

His father's brother had a villa there. His grandmother — Nain Åsa, though she was not yet Nain to anyone, was simply Åsa, was simply a woman in her fifties with friends across Sweden and the kind of social network that existed before social networks, the web of summer cottages and borrowed houses and the mutual understanding that Swedish summers were not to be wasted indoors — Åsa had a friend with a cottage on the island. One of them. The villa or the cottage. He didn't know which. He knew only that they had been there, once, his parents and their three small children, in the geological age before everything changed — the age when his parents had been completely different parents, when they had been young and married and functional and raising children with the help of extended family and borrowed kitchens and the long, light-drenched Scandinavian summers that made everything possible, that stretched the days until ten at night and filled them with the smell of grilled fish and the sound of children on grass and the particular Swedish silence that is not absence but fullness, the silence of a country that has enough space to be quiet.

The dream held him there. His father's arms. The wind. The cliffs.

It was not a long memory. He had told himself this, in the waking hours, on the occasions when the dream returned — which it did, sporadically, without warning, arriving in his sleep like a letter from a country he'd visited once and never returned to. It was not a long memory. A few seconds, maybe. A moment. The kind of moment that is so small it should not survive, that should be dissolved by the decades that followed, by the white room and the towels and the silence and the take care and the woman his mother became and the man his father stopped being. But it survived. It survived because the body is more faithful than the mind, and the body remembered his father's hands, and the wind, and the salt, and the cliffs that stood like sentinels at the edge of a world that had once, briefly, been whole.

Why this dream? Why tonight?

He didn't know. The dream didn't explain itself. It never did. It simply arrived, offered its few seconds of warmth, and withdrew — leaving him not with sadness, exactly, but with the particular ache of a man who knows what holding feels like because he was held, once, before the holding stopped.

He held Tima tighter.

She stirred, murmured something that was not a word but was more than silence, and pressed her face deeper into his chest, and the weight of her was the answer to the dream's question, and the answer was: because this is what it is for. The holding. This is what it is for.

# Part Two: Bore Da

A hand landed on his face.

Not a slap — Tima did not slap, she lacked the motor coordination for deliberate violence and the temperament for accidental cruelty — but a placement. A flat, open-palmed, matter-of-fact placement of a small hand across his nose and mouth, the gesture of a child who has rolled over in her sleep and whose arm, operating on its own authority, has decided that Papa's face is a reasonable place to rest.

Papa woke. The hand was warm and slightly damp and smelled of the soap from last night's bath — the lavender soap, the one Steph had given them, the one that came from the shop in Newport and that Papa privately suspected was not entirely soap, that it contained something else, some herb or intention or whatever it was that Steph put into things when she made them, the quality that turned her products from objects into — what had Peter called them? — small spells for people who don't know they need one.

He opened his eyes. Tima had rolled. She was on her back now, her arm flung across his face, her body arranged in the shape of a starfish — or a compass rose — every limb pointing in a different direction, claiming the maximum possible area of mattress. Hoppy had been pushed to the edge and was balanced on the precipice of the pillow, his one eye staring at the ceiling with the resigned expression of a toy who has accepted that proximity to Tima means a lifetime of being flung, dropped, posted behind sofas, and loved with a ferocity that leaves marks.

She was awake. He could tell because her eyes were open and tracking the light — the morning light, the August light that came through the gap in the curtains and threw a bright stripe across the bed. She was watching the stripe the way she watched everything — with that particular intensity, that full-bodied attention that made her look not like a child observing light but like a scientist conducting an experiment in which the variable was the world and the hypothesis was that the world was interesting. The hypothesis was always confirmed.

"Bore da, daffodil," Papa said, his voice muffled by her hand.

She turned her head. The storm blue eyes found him. The smile arrived — slow, sleepy, the sunrise version of a smile, the one that builds from nothing and ends with the whole face involved.

"Bore da, Tad," she said. And then, without removing her hand from his face: "You were sleeping."

"I was."

"For a long time."

"Was I?"

"Yes. I counted."

"How high did you count?"

She considered. "All the way."

Papa laughed — the particular laugh of a parent whose child has said something so perfectly, so completely itself, that the only response is joy. He scooped her up, one arm under her, lifting her the way he'd lifted her since she was born — the full-body scoop, the commitment of muscle, the motion that was, by now, as automatic as breathing. She went with it, loose-limbed and warm, her arms wrapping around his neck, her face pressed into the space beneath his ear that was hers and had always been hers, the exact spot where her cheek fit against his skin as if the spot had been built for the purpose.

"Breakfast?" he said.

"Pancakes."

"We don't have pancakes."

"Toast."

"We have toast."

"Toast with jam. And Hoppy wants toast too."

"Hoppy is a hippo. Hippos don't eat toast."

She pulled back to look at him with an expression of such withering scepticism that he almost apologised. "Hoppy eats everything, Tad. You know that."

He did know that. Hoppy's diet, as documented by Tima over the past six months, included toast, porridge, clouds, the colour yellow, and — on one memorable occasion — an entire imaginary fish that Tima had caught from the arm of the sofa using a spoon and a length of string.

They went downstairs. The cottage was cool — stone walls, low ceilings, the thermal character of a building that had been absorbing the valley's temperature for two hundred years and had settled on slightly chilly as its default setting, regardless of season. The stairs creaked in the usual places. The kitchen was as he'd left it — the plate from last night, the teacup in the sink, the window looking out onto the small back garden where the herbs were going wild and the grass needed cutting and the morning light lay across the stone wall like something painted there.

Papa made toast. The kettle went on. Tima sat at the table in her pyjamas with Hoppy propped against the milk bottle, his one eye facing the window as if he too wanted to know what the weather was doing.

"Is Peter coming today?" Tima asked, swinging her legs.

"I don't know, love. Peter's been at Steph's."

"With Eira?"

"With Eira."

"I want Eira to come."

"Maybe she will."

"I want Eira to come now."

"Eira doesn't come on command, Tima. She's not a bus."

Tima absorbed this. "Eira is better than a bus."

Papa set the toast down — cut into triangles, always triangles, the muscle memory of Peter's hands performing Peter's geometry, the habit inherited from a man who taught him that toast was not merely bread but a small, daily act of care whose shape mattered. Jam on one triangle, butter on another, the third one plain because Tima liked to decide and changing her mind was part of the deciding.

They ate. The kitchen was quiet except for the sound of toast being eaten by a toddler, which is not quiet at all — it is the sound of crumbs being distributed across every available surface by a creature who eats with her entire body, her mouth, her hands, her elbows, even her eyebrows involved in the consumption, the toast less eaten than experienced.

"Can we go outside?" Tima said, mid-bite.

"After breakfast."

"This is after breakfast."

"This is during breakfast."

"It's nearly after."

Papa sipped his coffee. She was right, technically. She was always right, technically. She had inherited his capacity for finding the angle that made the desired conclusion logically defensible, and she deployed it with the frequency and precision of a child who had discovered that adults, when confronted with sound reasoning, sometimes conceded.

The playground was close. Everything was close — that was the nature of Pontywaun, of this particular cluster of cottages on Hawthorn Place, the terraced houses connected like carriages on a train, each one slightly different from the next in the way that siblings are different, the same bones but different curtains. Their cottage had a small front garden — a patch, really, where Papa had put in herbs and a few flowers, the kind of garden that looks accidental but is not, the kind that a man who learned from Peter tends with the patience of someone who knows that growing things is its own reward. And a small back garden — a rectangle of grass, the stone wall, the bins, the view uphill toward the treeline where the forest began.

Just past the next cottage — Mrs Griffiths' cottage, the woman who watched from her window with the genial suspicion of a person who has lived in the same house for fifty years and considers all subsequent arrivals to be newcomers regardless of how long they stay — was the playground. Small. A swing. A sandbox. One of those spring-mounted animals that rock back and forth, its paint faded to the colour of mild enthusiasm. A bench for the parents. The ground was that rubbery surface that playgrounds had started using, the spongy black-and-green material that was supposed to cushion falls and instead collected water and made everything slightly damp.

Tima went for the sandbox. Of course she did — the sandbox was hers, had been hers since she was old enough to sit in it, the way certain places belong to certain people not through ownership but through the quality of attention they bring to them. She sat in the sand and began the work — the serious, concentrated, endlessly absorbing work of moving sand from one place to another, of filling a bucket and emptying it, of sifting the grains through her fingers and watching them fall. Hoppy was installed on the edge of the sandbox, his face pointed outward, the sentinel on the perimeter.

Papa sat on the bench with his coffee. The morning was warm already — August, the sun climbing above the ridge, the light falling through the trees at the angle that made everything look like a postcard of itself. A blackbird was singing from Mrs Griffiths' hedge. Somewhere down the hill, a car started. Somewhere further, the valley held its breath between one hour and the next.

His pocket buzzed.

The phone — the old Motorola, the grey brick with the cracked screen and the battery that lasted three days because it did nothing except make calls and receive texts, the phone of a man who had no interest in the internet and considerable interest in not being reachable by anyone except the people who mattered. He flipped it open.

Peter.

"Bore da, butt."

Papa smiled. Peter's butt — the South Welsh punctuation mark, the word that meant mate and friend and the person I'm talking to all at once, deployed with the warmth of a man who'd adopted Papa into his vocabulary the way he'd adopted him into his life, completely and without ceremony.

"Bore da. You're up early."

"Up early? I've been up since half five. Eira decided that dawn is a suggestion and five-thirty is a fact."

Papa laughed. "She gets that from Steph."

"She gets everything from Steph. Including the ability to make me do things before coffee." A pause. The sound of a kettle in the background, and a smaller voice — Eira's voice, bright and insistent, saying something about shoes. "Listen. Steph's been called in. They're short of staff — someone on the ward's off sick, they need a pair of hands."

"So she's working?"

"She's working. Which means I've got our little general here, and the little general has decided —" the word was loaded with the particular emphasis of a man who has been informed, not consulted — "that today is a family day. Her words. She wants Tima. She wants the forest. She wants — what was it, Eira?"

The smaller voice, closer now, louder: "I want to go UP."

Peter returned. "She wants to go up. The forest drive. Cwmcarn. Shall I bring a picnic?"

Papa looked at Tima in the sandbox. Tima was building something — what, he couldn't tell, the architecture of a two-and-a-half-year-old being subject to its own internal logic — but she was building it with the concentration of a woman laying bricks.

"Tima," he called. "Eira wants to come. Peter too. They want to walk up the forest. Shall we?"

Tima's head came up. The name Eira had the same effect it always had — the widening of the eyes, the smile, the full-body alert of a child who has heard the name of the person she considers to be the most important person in the world after Papa and Hoppy.

"Eira's coming?"

"If you want."

"YES."

Papa put the phone back to his ear. "She says yes."

"I heard. I think they heard in Newport."

"Bring the blanket. The tartan one."

"Obviously. I'm not a savage. Give us an hour."

"See you then."

He closed the phone. Tima was standing now, the sandbox abandoned, the architecture forgotten, her entire being oriented toward the road down which Eira would arrive.

"How long is an hour?" she asked.

"About the time it takes to build a really good sandcastle."

She looked at the sandbox. She looked at the road. She sat back down in the sand. The calculation had been made.

# Part Three: The Forest Drive

Peter's car announced itself before it appeared — the particular cough of an engine that had been driven hard through valleys and up hills for more years than the manufacturer had intended, the sound of a Toyota that had passed through reliable and ageing and was now in the territory of characterful, which is what people say about cars that work most of the time and make interesting noises the rest.

Tima heard it first. She was on her feet before the car rounded the corner, her hands gripping the playground fence, her body tipped forward on tiptoe, the posture of a child who would climb the fence if the laws of physics and parental prohibition allowed it.

The car pulled up. The passenger door opened before the engine was off.

Eira.

She emerged the way she always emerged — at speed, at volume, in motion before her feet hit the ground, her chestnut curls wild around a face that was already talking, already mid-sentence, already in the middle of a thought that had started in the car and would continue until the thought was finished or she fell asleep, whichever came first.

"TIMA! Tima, we're going to the FOREST and I brought crayons and Peter made sandwiches and I can carry the blanket because I'm STRONGEST and —"

Tima was through the playground gate and running. Not fast — she was never as fast as Eira, never had been, the chILDS made sure of that, the condition that sat in her chest like a guest who wouldn't leave, the thing that made her breathe a little harder on hills and sit out of running games and move through the world at a pace that was hers and not the pace the world preferred — but running, arms out, Hoppy bouncing in one hand, the smile so wide it rearranged her entire face.

They collided. Not a hug — a collision, the full-body impact of two small people who have been apart for days and whose bodies have not yet learned that greetings can be conducted at lower velocities. They went down in a tangle of limbs and curls and Hoppy and the sound of laughter that was so pure, so unself-conscious, so entirely the sound of joy that Papa, standing by the bench with his coffee, felt something shift in his chest that he did not name because naming it would have diminished it.

Peter appeared from the driver's side. He was carrying bags — plural, because Peter's definition of a picnic was closer to a military operation than a meal, the kind of preparation that involved multiple containers, a blanket, a thermos, backup sandwiches, apples, crisps, biscuits, and the implicit understanding that no one under his care would go hungry while there was breath in his body and bread in the boot.

"Morning," Peter said, setting the bags on the bench. He looked at Papa. The look was the look — the one that had been there since Wales, since the cottage, since the first morning Peter had watched Papa hold Tima and understood that some men are built for fatherhood the way some rivers are built for valleys. The look that said: you're doing alright, lad. The look that never needed to be spoken because Peter's face said it louder than words.

"Morning. How was the drive?"

"Eira sang. The entire way. One song. Repeated. Seventeen times."

"Which song?"

"I genuinely don't know. It may not be a song. It may be an incantation. Steph would know."

Papa grinned. Eira was sitting in the sandbox now, having relocated the entire operation within thirty seconds of arrival, and was explaining to Tima — with the patient authority of a child two years her senior — that the sandcastle needed a moat, and the moat needed water, and the water needed to come from somewhere, and where was the tap?

"There's no tap, Eira."

"Then we need to FIND water."

Peter rubbed his face with both hands, the gesture of a man who has been awake since five-thirty and whose day is only just beginning. "Shall we walk, then? Before she dismantles the playground."

The forest drive began at the end of the road. Not metaphorically — literally at the end, past the last cottage, past the trees that separated the houses from the hillside, past the gate that marked the boundary between the lived-in world and the other world, the forest world, the seven miles of road and trail that wound up the flanks of Twmbarlwm through woods that had been ancient, then felled, then planted, then felled again, the forest that kept coming back because forests do, because the ground remembers what grew there and grows it again.

They walked. Peter in front — he was always in front, not because he was fastest but because he navigated the way he did everything, with the body's full commitment, leaning into the hill, his walking stick (not Stick-Stick — that was Tima's, found on a different walk, a different day — but his own stick, the hazel one he'd cut and stripped himself, the handle worn smooth by his palm) tapping a rhythm on the road that the girls behind him matched without being asked.

Eira held Tima's hand. This was non-negotiable. Eira held Tima's hand on walks the way she held Tima's hand on everything — with the proprietary grip of a child who has decided that another child belongs to her and that the belonging is expressed through physical contact. She was taller. She was stronger. She was the engine, and Tima was the cargo, and the cargo was willing because the cargo adored the engine with a devotion that was absolute and uncomplicated.

"Look," Eira said, pointing at a mushroom on the verge. "A fairy house."

"It's a mushroom," Papa said.

"It's a fairy house shaped like a mushroom. Mummy says all the mushrooms in the forest are fairy houses."

Peter, without turning: "Your mother would know."

This was true. Steph would know. Steph knew things about forests that other people didn't — not in the academic sense, not the Latin names and the classifications, but in the other sense, the sense that comes from being a woman who keeps dried mugwort in jars and moon water on the windowsill and who has, on more than one occasion, walked into a forest and come back knowing something she didn't know before, the knowledge arriving not through reading but through — what? Listening. The kind of listening that Steph did, and Peter respected, and Papa was slowly learning to understand.

The road climbed. The houses fell away below them and the trees thickened — birch and oak and the occasional larch, the survivors of the felling, the ones that the disease hadn't taken. The canopy closed overhead and the light changed, softening, filtering through leaves into the green-gold wash that forests make of sunlight, the light that turns everything beneath it into something from a different century.

Tima walked well. She walked with that particular determination — the going-forward quality that characterised her movement, the stride that was slightly too wide for her legs and slightly too fast for her lungs but that she maintained because stopping was not something she did voluntarily. She breathed harder on the incline. Papa noticed. Papa always noticed — the way her breathing changed on hills, the way her shoulders rose, the way she compensated for the thing in her chest by working the rest of her body harder, the trade-off that the condition imposed and that she accepted with the uncomplaining pragmatism of a child who has never known any different.

"Want a carry, daffodil?"

"No."

"Sure?"

"I'm walking, Tad. Like Eira."

Like Eira. Always like Eira. The aspiration, the north star, the girl she was walking toward even when what she was walking toward was simply the next tree, the next turn, the next mushroom that was secretly a fairy house.

Peter slowed his pace. He did this without being asked, the way he did most things — the adjustment was invisible, a fraction of a step shorter, a pause at a bend disguised as appreciation of the view, the generosity of a man who understood that the group moves at the speed of its smallest member and that the smallest member's dignity is preserved by not making the slowness visible.

"Look at that," Peter said, standing at a gap in the trees where the valley opened below them. The view — the whole of it, the Ebbw valley spread out like a map of itself, the river glinting silver in the morning sun, the houses climbing the hillsides, the old colliery site where the forest drive ran over the shafts of mines that had been closed since the sixties, the green reclaiming everything, the stubborn Welsh green that covered scars the way moss covers stone, not by removing but by growing over.

Tima stopped beside him, breathing hard, and looked.

"It's big," she said.

"Aye," Peter said. "It is."

"Where's our house?"

Peter crouched, pointing. "See there? Where the road bends, just past the rooftops? That's us. That's Hawthorn Place."

Tima looked. She looked with the concentration she gave everything — the total, scanning, absorbing look of a child who is not merely seeing but recording, filing, adding the view to the expanding archive of the world that she was building inside herself.

"It's small," she said. "From here."

Peter nodded. "Most things are, from far enough away. That's why you've got to get close to know them proper."

They kept walking.

# Part Four: The Hilltop

The playground at the top of the forest drive was the kind that existed before safety was invented.

Not literally — the equipment was recent enough, the swings and the climbing frame and the roundabout with the chipped paint — but the spirit of it was older. It sat in a clearing where the trees fell back and the sky opened up and the ground was just grass, thick and rough, the kind of grass that council mowers attacked periodically but that grew back with the defiance of vegetation that has been growing in Welsh soil for longer than councils have existed. Below, the valley dropped away into the haze of distance. Above, the sky did what the Welsh sky does in August — it couldn't decide, offering sun and cloud in alternating measures, the atmospheric equivalent of a person who keeps changing their mind but whose indecision is, in itself, a kind of beauty.

Eira and Tima claimed the swings with the territorial efficiency of small children who have spotted playground equipment from fifty metres and who have been running — or in Tima's case, walking very fast — since the moment of sighting.

"Push me, Peter! PUSH ME."

"Push me too, Tad!"

Peter and Papa took up their stations behind the swings, and the pushing began, and the rhythm of it — the swing forward, the pause, the fall back, the push, the squeal, the swing forward again — was the oldest rhythm in the world, the rhythm that playgrounds make of children and gravity and the immovable fact that going up feels like freedom and coming down feels like trust.

"Higher!" Eira demanded.

"I'm not launching you into orbit, cariad."

"HIGHER."

Peter pushed higher. He pushed the way he did everything — with commitment, with the body behind it, the push of a man who had built walls and carried stone and whose strength had found, in his fifties, its gentlest application in the act of sending a small girl toward the sky and catching her at the bottom of the arc.

Tima's swing was lower. Papa pushed softer. Not because she couldn't handle height — she could, she was fearless in the way that children with conditions are often fearless, the condition teaching them early that the body is a thing to be negotiated rather than taken for granted, and the negotiation producing a relationship with risk that was not reckless but considered — but because the rhythm between them was different, slower, the rhythm of a man and a girl who had been synchronised since birth.

"Like flying," Tima said, her eyes wide, the wind in her hair.

"Like flying."

The picnic happened on the grass. Peter spread the tartan blanket — the big one, the one that had been in the boot of his car since approximately the beginning of time and that smelled of car and grass and something faintly like Peter himself, the particular blend of tea and boot leather and the outdoors that was his signature as surely as a cologne, which he would never wear. The bags were opened. The contents were arranged.

"Right," Peter said, surveying his work with the satisfaction of a man who has provisioned adequately. "Sandwiches — ham for the grown-ups, cheese for the commanders. Apples. Crisps. Biscuits for afters. Flask of tea. Flask of squash. Questions?"

"I want a biscuit first," Eira said.

"That's not a question, that's a demand."

"Please can I have a biscuit first?"

"Better. No. Sandwich first."

The girls ate. The adults ate. The food was simple and correct in the way that picnic food is always correct — the bread slightly compressed from the bag, the butter uneven, the ham folded rather than placed, the apple with the small bruise that proved it was real — and the eating was conducted in the particular style of outdoor eating with children, which is to say that approximately sixty percent of the food entered the intended mouths and the remaining forty percent was distributed across the blanket, the grass, and — in Hoppy's case — his remaining eye.

Papa lay back on the blanket. The sky above was doing its August performance — clouds building and dissolving, the sun appearing and disappearing, the light shifting across the hilltop like a hand moving across a face. Tima sat beside him, her legs crossed, her sandwich half-eaten, her attention split between the food and a beetle that was making its way across the blanket with the slow determination of a creature that has somewhere to be and does not care that it is crossing a tartan landscape populated by giants.

"Tell a story, Tad," she said, not looking at him, her eyes on the beetle.

"What kind of story?"

"A Sweden story."

Eira, who had been attempting to climb Peter's back while he sat cross-legged eating his sandwich, stopped mid-ascent. "I want a Sweden story too."

Peter, with a child hanging from his shoulders: "Go on then, lad. Give them a Sweden story."

Papa watched the beetle. He watched Tima watching the beetle. And something in the watching opened a door — the way it did sometimes, the way certain moments of quietness and warmth and the right kind of light could open a door inside him that led back, that led to the place before the white room and the towels and the silence, the place where Sweden was not a wound but a country, not a grief but a childhood.

"When I was a boy," he said, and the girls went still, because when I was a boy was the opening, the spell, the words that turned the blanket on the hilltop into a vessel and the afternoon into a journey, "I used to ride a train."

"What kind of train?" Tima asked.

"A slow train. The slowest train in Sweden. They called it the Inlandsbanan — the inland railway. It ran from the south all the way up to the far north, through forests and mountains and places where nobody lived, places where the land was so empty you could look out the window and see nothing but trees for hours."

"Hours?" Eira said, sceptical. Eira's world was contained within the valley, and hours of nothing was a concept she approached with suspicion.

"Hours," Papa confirmed. "But not boring hours. Different hours. The kind where you watch the world go by and it doesn't need anything from you. It just… shows itself."

"Who did you go with?" Tima asked.

"My grandmother. Nain. She'd take me in the summer — just the two of us. We'd pack a bag and get on the train and we'd go north, stopping whenever we felt like it. Sometimes at a town, sometimes at a station in the middle of nowhere, and we'd get off and walk."

"Walk where?"

"Wherever looked interesting. We'd find lakes. Rivers. Little shops that sold smoked fish and candy. Churchyards with stones so old you couldn't read the names anymore." He smiled. "She never had a plan. That was the thing about Nain. She'd been everywhere, knew people everywhere — if we needed a place to stay, she'd know someone. A friend with a cottage. A cousin with a spare room. Sweden's like that, up north. Everyone knows everyone, and everyone's door is open in summer."

Peter unwrapped a biscuit. "Sounds like Wales before telly."

Papa laughed. "Maybe. But colder. And the mosquitoes were the size of your fist."

"Now you're exaggerating," Peter said.

"Only slightly."

Tima had abandoned the beetle. She was leaning against Papa's chest now, her head on his ribs, looking up at the sky the way she looked at everything — with the total, absorbing attention that was her gift and her nature.

"What else?" she said. "More about the train."

"Well," Papa said, "there was the treasure hunting."

"Treasure?" Both girls, simultaneously.

"My sister Sofia and I — she's older than me, a couple of years — when we were small, maybe five and seven, we used to go treasure hunting. In Östersund, where we lived, and on Gotland, the island where my family went in summer. We'd spend entire days looking."

"For what?" Eira demanded, because Eira demanded everything.

"Bottle caps, mostly."

The girls stared at him.

"Bottle caps," Papa repeated, grinning. "We were convinced they were valuable. We'd walk through the streets, the parks, down by the lake, crouched over, staring at the ground, collecting every bottle cap we found. Sofia kept them in a tin. I kept mine in my pockets. We'd compare at the end of the day — who found the most, who found the best. The best was always the one that was the most unusual. A foreign brand. A colour you hadn't seen before. A cap that was bent in an interesting way."

Tima's face was serious. "Were they valuable?"

"Not to anyone else. But to us they were treasure. Real treasure. The kind that's valuable because you found it, not because someone told you it was worth something."

"Like kattguld," he added. "Fool's gold. Pyrite. We'd find it sometimes, by the lake, in the streams. Little flakes of gold in the sand and the rocks. We'd fill our pockets. We thought we were rich."

"Were you?" Tima asked.

Papa looked at her. At the storm blue eyes. At the dark curls. At the face that held everything he had ever lost and everything he had ever found.

"Yes," he said. "We were."

Peter was quiet. He was listening, from behind his tea, with the particular quality of attention that Peter gave to things that mattered — not the active listening of a man trying to gather information, but the receptive stillness of a friend who understands that some stories are being told not for the listener but for the teller, and the teller is a man who needs, sometimes, to remember that he had a childhood, that the childhood contained joy, that the joy was real even if everything that came after tried to erase it.

"Tell them about the hill," Peter said.

Papa looked at him. "You know about the hill?"

"You told me. Once. Late. After the girls were asleep. You'd had a whisky."

"I'd had three whiskies."

"Tell them."

Papa shifted, settling Tima more comfortably against his chest. Eira had come down from Peter's shoulders and was lying on the blanket, her chin on her fists, her face open and waiting.

"There was a hill," Papa said. "Behind our house in Lit — the village north of Östersund where we lived for a while. A yellow radhus, a terraced house, with a small garden. And behind it, a hill. Not a big hill. Not a mountain. Just a hill, covered in grass, the kind of grass that's thick and long in summer and turns gold in autumn."

"What did you do on it?"

"Everything. On summer days — the long summer days, when the sun doesn't set until ten at night — I'd lie in the grass and watch the sky. Just lie there. The grass smelled like… like warmth, like the sun had eaten the grass and the grass was breathing it back. You could hear the crickets. The bees. The wind going through the trees at the bottom. And the sky was so big — big the way the sky is in places where there's nothing to interrupt it — just blue, forever, in every direction."

He paused. The memory was there — not painful, not weighted, just there, vivid and warm, the kind of memory that survives because it is simple and because simple things, it turns out, are the hardest to destroy.

"And in autumn," he said, "we'd burn the grass."

"Burn it?" Eira sat up. "Actually burn it?"

"Actually burn it. Everyone did. It was a thing — before winter, you'd burn the old grass to clear the ground for spring. Not a wildfire. A controlled burn. My father would set it and we'd stand back and watch it go — the flames moving through the grass in a line, crackling, the smoke rising straight up into the October sky. And after, the hill was black, completely black, and it smelled like — I don't know. Like endings. Like something making room for something else."

Tima, quiet through all of this, said: "Did the grass come back?"

Papa kissed the top of her head. "Always," he said. "It always came back."

# Part Five: The Philanthropic

The walk down was easier than the walk up. It always is — gravity doing the work that the legs did on the way up, the body leaning back instead of forward, the pace quicker, looser, the rhythm of descent. The forest closed around them and then opened again and then the houses appeared below, the rooftops of Pontywaun and Crosskeys arranged along the valley floor like boats moored in a harbour.

Eira and Tima walked ahead. Eira was talking — she was always talking, the narrative unbroken, a continuous stream of observation and opinion and the occasional proclamation that required no response but received one anyway because Tima, walking beside her, holding her hand, said "yes" and "okay" and "look" with the reliable enthusiasm of a person who has found her person and whose person can do no wrong.

Tima was tired. Papa could see it — the way her steps shortened, the way her shoulders dropped, the way she leaned into Eira's grip rather than walking beside it. The hill had cost her. Every hill cost her. The condition sat in her chest and levied its tax and she paid it without complaint, the way she paid everything, with the quiet mathematics of a child who has learned to budget her breath.

Papa caught Peter's eye. Peter nodded — the nod that contained an entire conversation: she's tired, she won't admit it, don't ask, just adjust. Papa adjusted. He lengthened his stride to close the gap and crouched.

"Hop on, daffodil."

For once, she didn't argue. She climbed onto his back — arms around his neck, legs around his waist, Hoppy pressed between her chest and his spine — and the weight of her settled against him, and he stood, and the walk continued, and she rested.

Peter walked beside him. The stick tapping. The valley below. The afternoon tilting toward evening, the light going gold, the shadows lengthening under the trees.

"Good day," Peter said.

"Good day."

"She liked the Sweden stories."

"She likes all stories."

"She likes your stories." Peter glanced at him. "You should tell them more."

Papa didn't answer, but the silence was agreement.

The Philanthropic Inn sat on the corner where Pontywaun met the road, the kind of pub that every Welsh valley village has — stone walls, low windows, a sign that had been repainted enough times that the current version was a palimpsest of every version that came before. The Philanthropic. The name was aspirational in the way that Victorian pub names were aspirational — the builders believed that naming a pub after a virtue might instil the virtue in its patrons, a theory that had been tested by a hundred and fifty years of darts and bitter and had produced, at best, mixed results.

They went in. The pub was warm and dim and smelled of the particular blend of spilled ale, wood polish, and carpet that constitutes the British pub's signature perfume. A fire was going — unnecessary in August, but the Philanthropic kept a fire the way churches keep candles, as a statement of identity rather than a response to temperature. The bar was dark wood. The taps were brass. Three men at the far end were watching a football match on a television mounted too high on the wall, their pints at various stages of depletion.

And someone was playing. A small stage — barely a stage, more a slightly raised area of floor near the window — held a guitar case, an amp, and two people who were either setting up or packing down, it was hard to tell. One of them strummed a chord. The sound was warm and slightly out of tune, the sound of a pub band that has been playing the same songs in the same pub for years and has achieved that particular state of competence where the music is not good, exactly, but is right — right for the room, right for the evening, right for the pints and the fire and the particular quality of a Saturday late afternoon in a South Welsh valleys pub.

Peter went to the bar. He returned with two pints — one for each of them — and two glasses of something for the girls. Lemonade, probably, or one of those fruit drinks that comes in bottles shaped like animals, the provenance of which is unclear but the sugar content of which is beyond dispute.

"Right," Peter said, setting the glasses down. "Darts?"

The girls were installed at a table near the window. Eira had produced the crayons — promised in the car, delivered now — and was already drawing, her head bent over the paper, her tongue between her teeth, the absolute concentration of a child engaged in creative work. Tima sat beside her, drawing too, though Tima's drawing was more abstract — spirals and lines and colours placed without representational intent, the drawing of a child who is interested in the act of making marks rather than the marks themselves.

Hoppy sat between them, presiding.

Papa and Peter threw darts. Papa was better — his hand was steadier, his aim more precise, the product of a childhood spent throwing things at targets in Scandinavian forests rather than Cornish pubs — but Peter was more committed, the way Peter was more committed to everything, each dart thrown with the full weight of his body behind it, the dart leaving his hand with a velocity that was impressive and a trajectory that was unpredictable.

"Twenty!" Peter announced, pulling his dart from the board.

"That's the five, Peter."

"It's near the twenty."

"Near isn't in."

"In spirit, it's in."

The band started playing. Something that might have been a Stereophonics song, filtered through the particular interpretation of two men with one guitar and a box drum and the complete confidence of people who have decided that momentum is more important than accuracy. The music filled the pub the way firelight fills a room — not by illuminating everything but by changing the quality of the everything, by making the mundane warmer, the ordinary softer, the Saturday afternoon into something that would, years from now, be remembered not for its events but for its feeling.

Tima looked up from her drawing. She was listening. Her head tilted — the way it did, the way it always did when something caught the particular channel of her attention, the tilt of a child tuning in to a frequency that interested her. Her feet, dangling from the chair, began to swing in a rhythm that was not quite the rhythm of the music but was adjacent to it, the rhythm of a two-and-a-half-year-old's relationship with time, which is approximate, which is musical in intent if not in execution.

"I like this song," she said.

Eira, without looking up: "It's not a real song. Real songs have drums."

"It has drums."

"That's a box."

"A box can be drums."

Eira considered this. The logic was sound. She returned to her drawing.

Papa watched them. He watched from the dartboard, a dart in his hand, the pub around him, the music playing, Peter beside him arguing with the scoring — Peter, who would argue with a clock about the time — and the girls at the table, drawing simultaneously, their heads bent at the same angle, their crayons moving with the same focused intensity, the two of them so close that their elbows touched and neither noticed because the touching was as natural as breathing, as ordinary as the air between their faces, as unremarkable and as essential as anything could be.

He threw the dart. It landed in the twenty.

"Show-off," Peter said.



The evening came down like a curtain, the slow August descent, the light fading from the pub windows in stages — gold, then amber, then the particular blue-grey of a valley dusk. They finished their pints. The girls finished their drawings — Eira had produced a portrait of Peter that featured a walking stick, an enormous head, and the word PETR across the top in letters that were creative in their spelling and confident in their size. Tima had produced a field of colour that she announced was "the hill, Tad, with the fire," and Papa folded it carefully and put it in his pocket where it would stay, through washes and years and the things that years bring, until the paper was soft as cloth and the colours had faded to ghosts and the drawing was no longer a drawing but an artifact, a relic, a thing the world would not know the value of and that he would know the value of forever.

They walked home. The street was quiet. The houses glowed from within, each window a small rectangle of warmth in the gathering dark. Tima was on Papa's back again, her arms around his neck, her cheek against his shoulder. Eira walked between Peter's legs, holding both his hands, being swung forward with every other step, her laughter the only sound on the street apart from their footsteps and the distant, eternal murmur of the Ebbw below.

Home. The cottage. The door opening onto the hall that smelled of stone and the herbs from the garden and the particular domesticity of a house that has been lived in and loved in and that holds, in its walls and its floors and its particular creak on the third stair, the accumulated evidence of every day it has contained.

Bath. Not a long bath — the girls were too tired for a long bath, the day having used them up the way good days use children up, completely and well. Eira and Tima in the tub together, the water warm, the bubbles present because bubbles were non-negotiable, Eira washing Tima's hair with the careful, proprietary attention of a child who has decided that another child's hygiene is within her jurisdiction.

"Hold still, Tima."

"It's in my eyes."

"It's not in your eyes. I'm being careful."

"EIRA."

"There. Done. See? Not in your eyes."

Papa leaned against the bathroom doorframe. Peter was downstairs making tea, the kettle's whistle rising through the floor. The bathroom was small and steamy and the two girls were in the tub, pink and wet and laughing at something that only they found funny, some private joke between them, some fragment of a shared language that existed only in the space between their faces when they looked at each other and understood, without words, that they were each other's.



The big bed.

Both girls, clean and pyjama'd, installed in the centre of the bed that was, by now, more theirs than Papa's. Tima on the left. Eira on the right. Hoppy between them, his one eye facing the ceiling, his role as the bed's mediator and protector unchanged by the addition of a second child.

Papa sat on the edge. Peter leaned in the doorway, tea in hand, the fire from the grate downstairs sending a warm amber glow up the stairwell.

"Story," Eira said. Not a request.

"One," Papa said.

"One LONG one."

"One medium one."

Eira negotiated with her eyes. Papa held firm. Eira conceded. This was the rhythm of their exchanges — Eira pushing, Papa holding, the compromise arriving at a point that both of them knew was the correct point but that required the negotiation because the negotiation itself was the thing, the dance, the proof that care was being taken.

Papa told a story. Not a Sweden story — not tonight. Tonight it was a valley story, a forest story, something about a fox who lived under a bridge and who collected things that the river brought — not treasures, not gold, but sounds. The sound of rain on water. The sound of a child laughing upstream. The sound of a stone being thrown and the splash it made and the rings that spread outward, carrying the throw's intention into the wider world. The fox collected these sounds and kept them in a hollow beneath the roots and on quiet nights, when the valley was asleep, the fox would take them out and play them, one by one, and the valley would hear itself being remembered.

Eira was asleep before the fox reached the second sound. Her breathing had changed — the sudden, absolute transition of a child who has been running on fumes for the last hour and whose body, having received a horizontal surface and a warm bed, has simply switched off.

Tima was still awake. Her eyes were open — those storm blue eyes — and she was looking at Papa with the look she gave him sometimes, the look that was not a child looking at a parent but something older and quieter, the look of a person who knows something without knowing how they know it.

"Tad?"

"Yes, daffodil?"

"Was the hill real?"

"Which hill?"

"Your hill. The one with the grass and the fire."

"Yes, love. It was real."

She considered this. Her hand found Hoppy's ear and held it, the reflex that meant she was thinking, the grip that meant the thought was big.

"Can we go there?"

Papa looked at her. The question sat between them, small and enormous, the kind of question that a two-and-a-half-year-old asks because her world is still a place where distance is negotiable and geography is a matter of wanting, where can we go there means I want to see the place that made you and the wanting is pure and the place is far and the far is, for a child, simply a thing to be crossed.

"Someday," he said. "I'll take you someday."

"Promise?"

"Promise."

She smiled — the small smile, the closing-down smile, the one that arrives at the edge of sleep when the face is too tired to produce the full version but the feeling is there anyway, contained in the corners, in the slight lift, in the eyes that were closing now, finally, the lashes meeting, the storm blue disappearing behind them.

"Night night, Tad."

"Nos da, daffodil."

He kissed her forehead. He kissed it again. He pulled the duvet up over both of them — Tima and Eira, side by side, the dark curls and the chestnut curls on the same pillow, Hoppy between them — and he stood, and he stood there, and he didn't move, because the moment was the kind that does not ask to be left.

Peter, in the doorway, said nothing. He sipped his tea. The house settled around them — the creak of pipe, the tick of stone cooling, the valley outside holding its breath between one day and the next.

Papa looked at Peter.

Peter raised his mug. The smallest gesture. The quietest toast.

Papa stepped out of the room, pulling the door almost shut, leaving a crack for the landing light to find its way in — the stripe of light across the floor that Tima would open her eyes to if she woke, the light that told her she was home, that the home was here, that the here was enough.

They went downstairs. The fire was low. The tea was warm. The night was beginning outside the windows, the valley arranging itself for sleep, the Ebbw singing its song to no one and everyone, the song that carries names and stories and the small, bright weight of a day that was ordinary and good and will not be forgotten.

Papa sat in the armchair. Peter sat in his chair. The fire between them, the silence between them, the silence that was not absence but presence — the particular presence of two men who have spent a day with the people they love and who need nothing from the evening except the evening itself.

"Same time next week?" Peter said.

Papa smiled. "Same time next week."

And outside, the valley breathed. And the river carried what it carried. And the light in the upstairs window stayed on.
